Prose Ada
Chapter 2 (Draft 6)

1

Wade had already left when she got in. His notes were in the log, three lines, nothing about the shutter.

Jonas was at the desk with the weather up and a sleeve of crackers by the keyboard.

"Hey," he said. "Feeling better?"

"A little."

"You didn't text."

"Sorry. I fell asleep as soon as I got in."

"Figured." He scrolled the radar back an hour and let it play forward. "Holloway called. She's not coming up tonight. Same fields as last night, she said, and to mark anything bad."

"Okay."

She put her bag down and sat. She had slept in her jacket and woken with the light already going, and the day since then had been mostly showering and not eating. She hadn't been confused about what had happened to her. She knew what it had been like. She could call it up any time she let herself: how long it went on, long enough to get tired of being afraid and then be afraid again; how everything had been stacked on top of itself and not lined up; how the whole stack kept tearing and folding back and starting over from a little earlier, so that she lived the same stretch of seconds again and again and never got to the end of them. What she didn't know was what it was. She'd spent the drive in trying not to think about it, and then thinking about it anyway.

Jonas opened the dome and started the twilight flats. While the first frames came in, she opened the directory from the night before.

It was an ordinary thing to do. People pulled up old frames all the time. Even so, she didn't mention it, and she turned the monitor a couple of inches away from his side of the desk before she started.

Holloway's first field was there, a long run of five-minute exposures, each its own file, with a header of text at the top and the image underneath. She opened the header of the first and found the start time. Like every observatory she'd ever worked at, this one kept its clocks in universal time, which this time of year ran four hours ahead of local. Twenty oh-four on the clipboard would be four minutes past midnight in the files, and the date in the header would already have rolled over to today.

She went down the list until two start times bracketed it. One frame had opened a couple of minutes before and run on past.

She opened it.

At full view it looked like any other frame, a grey scatter of stars on black. Then she zoomed in.

Every star had broken into copies. Two or three or four faint duplicates sat a few pixels off from each one. Some of the copies were nearly as bright as the star they came from, and some were barely there.

She opened the frame before it. Round stars, flat sky. She opened the frame after it. The same.

She sat back and looked at it.

The first thing that came to mind was the obvious thing. She'd been up on the catwalk at exactly that time. She'd had her hands on the shutter rail. If she'd leaned on something, or the shutter had jumped while she was up there, the vibration could have run down through the building into the pier and shaken the telescope during the exposure. That would smear the stars. A sharp knock might even leave them doubled, the telescope jumping and settling, jumping and settling.

It was a perfectly good explanation. She'd have offered it to anyone else.

She opened the guider log. The guide camera had been watching one star the whole time, taking a short exposure every couple of seconds and nudging the telescope to keep that star in place. If the telescope had been knocked, the guide star should have jumped, and the log should show a big correction.

The corrections in those five minutes were as small as the ones on either side of them. That didn't settle it. A knock could come and go between two guide exposures. But it was the first thing that didn't fit.

She went back to the frame and looked for two stars close together. If the telescope shook, every star in the picture shook with it, the same distance and the same direction, the way every coin on a tray moves when you bump the tray. She found a pair near the middle. The copies of the first star ran off to the left. The copies of the second ran up and to the right.

She looked at that for a while.

She closed the frame and opened the night's schedule instead, and tried to think about Holloway's fields. The first one wouldn't be high enough for another half hour. She checked the camera temperature. She read Wade's three lines again. She looked at the radar, which was empty, and at the flats coming in one after another, each a little longer than the last.

It didn't work. Every few minutes she found she'd gone back to the pair of stars without meaning to, the copies of one running left and the other running up and right, and to the guider log that said nothing had happened. She told herself it would keep until morning. It was one frame. Holloway would never even look at it. Then she'd catch herself working out what she'd need to test it properly and how long it would take, and it was obvious she wasn't going to get through a whole night sitting next to it and doing nothing.

There was a flash drive in the front pocket of her bag. She plugged it in under the desk and copied the bad frame, the frames on either side, the guider log and the night's weather log, and when it was done she put the drive in her jacket pocket.

Jonas was eating crackers and watching the flats come in.

"Hey," she said. "I'm sorry, I'm still sick. I have to go home."

He turned around. "Oh. Yeah, okay." He looked at the schedule on the other screen, all of Holloway's fields lined up one after another into the morning, and rubbed the back of his head. "No, that's fine. Go. You looked kind of rough coming in."

"Sorry to leave you with all of it."

"It's fine. I'll manage." He was already pulling the schedule over to his side. "Feel better. Text me this time."

"I will."

2

She went home just long enough to get the laptop and the charger.

The diner was at the edge of town where the main road bent down toward the harbor. It was about half full: two men in a booth talking about a boat, an older couple splitting a plate of fried clams, someone at the counter reading the paper. The radio behind the counter was on low.

Ben was hunched over the counter with the diner phone, an old office-style handset on a curly cord, wedged between his shoulder and his ear while he wrote an order on a pad. He was in his usual dark T-shirt and jeans with an apron tied over them, dark hair pushed back off his forehead and in need of a cut, the lean build of someone who was on his feet all day. He saw her come in, raised his pen in a small wave, and kept writing.

"Hi, Benny," she said quietly as she went by. "I'm taking the back."

He nodded and said into the phone, "And that's with the coleslaw?"

The back table was against the wall by the kitchen door. It had the one outlet in the room that worked, and the Wi-Fi was best there because the router was on the other side of the wall. Nobody who came in to eat wanted to sit next to the kitchen door, so it was usually free. She'd done a lot of work at that table over the years.

She plugged in and opened the bad frame in the viewer she always used, the same free program she'd had on every computer since graduate school. 

After a few minutes Ben came back with a mug and set it down by her elbow.

"So what's the occasion? I don't usually get you on a weeknight."

"Sorry, Benny. I'm not really in a talking mood. I'm just really focused on something."

"Fair enough." He put a couple of creamers next to the mug. "I'll leave you to it."

She opened a blank document next to the image and started a list. When something came out wrong, you wrote down everything that could have caused it and crossed them off one at a time. She'd been taught that early, by a professor who said that the explanation you liked was always the last one you'd checked.

1. Telescope moved (shake, guiding slip)
2. Camera / electronics
3. Clock or log wrong
4. Something in the sky (satellite, plane, cloud)
5. Someone else saw it
6. Something bigger (magnetic field?)
7. Known effect she just didn't know about

She looked at the last one for a while. Then she added an eighth line, and left it blank.

3

She started with the telescope, because it was the likeliest and because she'd already half answered it.

Two stars weren't enough to go on. Two stars could fool you. What she wanted was every star in the frame, because each way a telescope could go wrong left its own pattern. If the whole telescope jumped, every star's copies would sit off in the same direction by the same amount. If the camera twisted in its mount, every star would turn by the same small angle around the middle of the picture, like a record turning, so the copies would sit sideways from their stars, short near the center and longer toward the edges. If the focus slipped, they'd all point in toward the center or out from it. However it moved, the movement would be smooth across the frame, and neighbors would agree with each other.

So she needed to know, for every star, where its copies had gone. She ran the frame through the star-finding program she'd used for years, which went through an image and listed every point of light it could find. It found a little under two thousand stars in the clean frame and nearly five thousand in the bad one, since it had no way of knowing the copies weren't stars.

To pair each star with its copies she dug out an old script of her own, written years ago for lining up frames from a different telescope. Getting it to work took longer than the matching did. It expected files named the way that other telescope named them, and when she fixed that it broke somewhere else, and when she fixed that it drew every arrow pointing backwards. Eventually it worked, and she had what she wanted: a small arrow on the image for every star, running from the star to its brightest copy.

The arrows were turning. Around most of the frame they ran sideways to their stars, as if everything had been given a twist. But the twist wasn't centered on the middle of the picture. It was centered on a point down and to the left of it, out in an empty patch of sky, and it wasn't the same twist everywhere. Close to that point the arrows were long and swung hard. Farther out they got shorter and leaned less, and near the far edges they barely turned at all. A camera twisting in its mount would have done the opposite. Every star would have turned by the same angle, and the arrows would have grown longer the farther they were from the center.

Each star had more than one copy, so she changed the drawing to join them up. Instead of one arrow per star, it drew a thin line from each star through each of its copies in order, the way they stepped away from it. She ran it and sat back.

The lines were curved. Near that point in the empty sky they curled tightly, and farther out they opened into long, shallow arcs, all bending the same way. Together they made a spiral, a slow pinwheel of thin lines laid over the stars. It looked like water going down a drain.

She had made a lot of diagnostic plots in her life, and none had ever looked like anything. They looked like a lot of arrows. She kept looking at this one. She followed one of the outer arcs in toward the middle and found herself doing it again with the next one, and then the one after that.

"Top you off?"

She shut the laptop. She did it before she'd thought about it, quickly enough that the lid clapped.

Ben stood there with the coffee pot. He looked at the closed laptop, then at the mug, and filled the mug.

"Don't worry," he said. "I can't read anyway." He went back to the counter.

She sat for a second with her hand flat on the lid. Then she opened it again.

The picture was still there. She made herself put it aside and do it properly, because a spiral that looked striking was still just something she'd drawn. She worked out the single best movement that could explain all the arrows, any mix of shifting, twisting and resizing, took it away, and looked at how much was left unexplained. If the telescope or the camera had moved, almost nothing should be left. Nearly all of it was left. A rigid twist couldn't make a spiral that was tight in one spot and loose everywhere else. For comparison she ran the same test on two ordinary frames from an hour apart, where the only difference was the usual wobble of the air. Those came out about a hundred times cleaner.

Whatever had happened, the telescope hadn't moved to make it. She crossed off the first line.

Across the room the older couple were splitting the last of the clams, the man pushing the bigger piece over to her side of the plate without looking up from what he was saying. She watched them for a minute. Ben came through the kitchen door with two plates and took them to the boat men.

The second line was the camera. A detector could misbehave in its own ways. Through a fault in the electronics, a bright star could leak a faint copy of itself onto another part of the chip, a little like a bad phone line picking up someone else's call. But a copy like that would be extra. It would be added on top of the real star, and the star wouldn't lose anything to make it.

That was easy to test. She measured how bright each star was in the clean frame, then measured the same star in the bad frame with its copies included. If the electronics were inventing the copies, the bad frame would come out brighter. If real light had simply landed in several places, the totals would match.

They matched, star after star, within the usual noise. Nothing had been added. The light had come down the telescope like it always did and arrived in several places instead of one. The camera had only recorded what reached it.

She crossed off the second line, and drank some of the coffee, and looked out the front window at the road for a while before she went on.

The third was quick. The camera's clock checked itself against a time server all night and had been off by a few thousandths of a second. It agreed with the guider log and with the time Jonas had written on the clipboard. The frame was taken when it said it was.

The fourth line had three things on it, and none of them held up for long. A satellite crossing the field would have left a straight streak, and it would only have touched the stars along its path. She went over the whole frame and found no streak. A plane would have left a dotted trail from its blinking lights, and there wasn't one. Cloud would have dimmed the stars, not split them, and she'd just shown that no light was missing. The weather log said clear and calm all night anyway.

She crossed it off and looked at the four lines with lines through them. She'd started the list half expecting to find something embarrassing in the first ten minutes. She hadn't.

4

Line five was whether anyone else had seen it. If something had really happened in the sky at that moment, it shouldn't exist only in one exposure at one small observatory in Maine.

She thought it through before she went looking. Four minutes past midnight universal time. On the West Coast it had still been daylight; the big survey telescopes in California and Arizona wouldn't have opened yet. In Europe it had been the small hours, but their telescopes would have been pointed at their own sky, nowhere near Holloway's field. She could think of no instrument that would have been looking at the same patch of sky at the same moment.

She didn't want to be too quick about that, so she spent a while pulling up whatever public observing schedules and survey logs she could find for that night. Most observatories didn't post them. The ones that did had been closed, clouded out, or pointed somewhere else. She'd been right the first time. She crossed it off, but she put a question mark next to it, because "nobody was looking" wasn't the same as "nothing happened."

Line six she'd written as a guess. There were instruments that watched all the time and didn't need to be pointed anywhere. The ones she knew best were the magnetometers, stations around the world that measured the Earth's magnetic field every second and posted the numbers publicly. A sharp disturbance in the field, from the sun or from something stranger, might show up there. She'd used that data once in graduate school to rule out a magnetic storm for a paper that had nothing to do with storms. What she remembered most was how plain the files were: a few lines of header, then column after column of numbers, one line per second.

It took her a while to find the right site again and work out which stations were closest. One in Virginia had already posted the day. She downloaded the file and, out of habit, opened it as plain text to look before doing anything clever with it. Eighty-six thousand lines, one for every second of the day, each a time and a few numbers. She started scrolling down toward the right minute by hand, which was a mistake; it took forever, and the numbers looked the same the whole way.

So she found an old script of hers that read files like these and drew them as lines over time, spent twenty minutes figuring out why it was reading every column one place off, and plotted the hour around the event.

At four minutes past midnight there was nothing.

She looked at the whole night, then looked specifically for anything sudden, a jump from one second to the next. Nothing stood out from the ordinary jitter.

She sat back.

She had wanted it to be there, which was a reason to distrust the wanting. It hadn't been there, which wasn't proof of anything either. The station was hundreds of miles away. Whatever had happened might have been small. She crossed off the line, lightly.

The boat men had paid and gone. The man reading the paper at the counter folded it, unfolded it to another page, and started on that. She watched him do it twice.

Ben stopped on his way past. "Kitchen's open another hour if you want anything."

"I'm okay. Thanks."

"Okay. Toast is free for people who look at spreadsheets." He went back to the counter.

5

Line seven was the one she'd been putting off: that this was a known effect, written up somewhere, and she just hadn't heard of it.

That was a real possibility. Nobody knew every way an instrument could go wrong. Somewhere there might be a two-page note from some observatory describing exactly this, and a sensible explanation, and once she read it she'd feel silly for an afternoon and get on with her life.

She opened the literature database, the one every astronomer she knew kept open in a browser tab, and searched it for every way she could think of to describe what she was seeing: multiple images, duplicate images, ghost images.

Most of what came back she could rule out from what she already knew. Gravitational lensing could show one object in several places, but only when a whole galaxy sat in the way, bending the light of something far behind it, never for ordinary nearby stars. Optical ghosts, faint reflections bouncing around inside the camera, were real and she'd seen plenty, but they always sat in the same spot beside a bright star and never touched faint ones. Then came a stack of papers about wind shake and mirror supports, which was the telescope again.

What was left was two odd reports, one about a camera shutter in the 1950s that bounced and one about a detector in the 1990s that echoed bright stars. She read both all the way through, hoping. Neither looked like hers.

Then she stopped and took her hands off the keyboard.

It was possible that the frame was real and still meant nothing. There could be some rare thing in the sky or in the air that nobody she knew had ever run into, and the one time it landed on a detector she happened to be up on the catwalk having whatever she'd had. Two unrelated things in the same minute. That happened. It happened all the time, and people built careers on not noticing it.

She tried to make that fit, and couldn't. Every check said the light had been in several places at once, with nothing moving it there. That was as close to a description of what had happened to her on the catwalk as anything she could have written. Calling it a coincidence would mean ignoring the one piece of evidence she'd been there for.

She left line seven open and looked at the blank eighth line.

The trouble with searching for this was that nobody kept bad frames now. A modern survey threw out thousands a night, automatically, and nobody looked at them or wrote anything about them. But for most of a century, astronomers had photographed the sky on glass plates, one at a time. A plate cost money and an hour of somebody's night, so people didn't throw them away. And when something went wrong, they wrote it down.

The biggest collection was at Harvard: something like half a million plates, many taken by small patrol cameras that photographed the whole sky over and over, from the 1880s on. She knew they'd been scanned and put online. Everyone had heard about it. She'd never actually used it.

She wrote "glass" on the eighth line.

It took a while to learn the archive. It could find plates by where in the sky and when, but not by what was wrong with them, and her first try, looking up Holloway's patch of sky, just gave her a few hundred perfectly good old pictures of the same stars. She did notice the dates thinning out almost to nothing through the 1950s and '60s, and looked it up: a director had stopped most of the plate-taking to save money. She made a note not to read anything into that stretch.

The archive also had the logbooks. The people taking the plates had kept notebooks of what they exposed and when, what the weather did and what went wrong, and volunteers had typed up many of the pages so they could be searched.

She tried "double" and got thousands, almost all double exposures made on purpose. "Doubled images" gave her fewer. "Images double, cause unknown" gave her seven.

She opened them one at a time.

The first two were plates where the clock drive that turned the camera with the sky had slipped. The stars made matching dashes across the whole plate, all pointing the same way. That was a slip, and it looked like one. The third had been bumped, and every star had the same twin the same distance off.

The fourth was a patrol plate from a night in March 1937, an hour's exposure. The scan loaded slowly. The glass was grey and pitted, with a crack across one corner. She zoomed in toward one edge. The star there had a faint companion off to one side. She went to the next star over. Its companion was on the other side.

She didn't let herself think anything about it yet. She put the plate through the same test as her own frame, because one plate that looked right meant nothing until she'd been as hard on it as she'd been on hers. She marked as many stars as she could make out on the old glass, drew the arrows and looked for any single movement that could explain them.

There wasn't one. When she joined the copies up, the lines curled around a point on the plate, tight near it and loosening farther out. It wasn't as clean as her frame, because old emulsion was grainier than a modern detector. But it was the same spiral.

Then she read the logbook page.

The handwriting was careful and slanted, mostly plate numbers and exposure times. Next to that plate the observer had written more than usual, and the volunteer's typed version sat under the scan:

Images double, cause unknown. Drive checked & found true. Sky clear throughout. At about the middle of the exposure I had a strong impression of the sky not keeping its place, as though it had been jarred, though nothing was. I record this only because I did not like it.

There were initials after it. The volunteer had marked them as unclear.

She read it three times.

The record linked to a scan of the plate's paper envelope, front side only. It was mostly a printed form, with the plate number and date filled in by hand. Along the bottom edge was a line of writing in a different pencil that ran off the scan, so only the tops of a few letters showed.

At the top of the page, the archive noted that the plate was held in the collection.

She sat and looked at that line for a while.

6

The kitchen got quiet. The couple with the clams paid and left, and then the boat men. Ben turned the radio down and then off and started putting chairs up on the tables in the front.

A while later he came back with a to-go box, a big one, and set it next to her laptop. It was full of fries.

"Shut the fryer down," he said. "That's what was left. It was going in the trash otherwise."

"Thanks."

He went back into the kitchen and came out again with a slice of boysenberry pie on a plate and put that down too. "Last piece."

"Benny, you don't have to—"

"It's the last piece. Somebody's got to eat it."

He went back to closing. She ate while she worked, some of the fries and most of the pie, and he did the dishes and wiped down the counter and mopped the front. She didn't notice how long it took.

When she looked up he was standing at the end of her table with his jacket on and his keys in his hand.

"I've got to lock up," he said.

"Sorry. Yeah." She started saving things.

He pulled out the other chair and sat down while she did it.

"So what's all this?" he said.

"Just a work thing."

"You've been back here five hours."

"Something weird came up in the data last night. One of the pictures. I'm trying to figure out what caused it."

"Huh." He nodded slowly. "Any luck?"

"Some. Not really."

"Benny, can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Last night, a little after eight. Did you feel anything weird?"

He frowned. "Weird how?"

"I don't know. Anything. Did anything seem off. Or did you see anything in the sky."

"I was in the kitchen most of the night. I don't see the sky from in there." He looked at her. "What do you mean, feel anything? Like an earthquake?"

"Not exactly."

"Then what?"

"I don't know. That's sort of why I'm asking."

He was quiet for a second.

"No," he said. "I didn't feel anything. Nobody said anything either. Why? What happened?"

"Nothing happened. I'm just checking."

"Ada." He leaned his arms on the table. "You don't usually ask stuff like that. You come in on a weeknight, you sit back here with your computer till close, and now you're asking me if I felt something."

"I know how it sounds."

"I'm not saying how it sounds. I'm asking if you're okay."

"I'm okay. I'm just tired."

"Okay." He didn't sound convinced. "Is it something with work? Somebody giving you a hard time?"

"No. Nothing like that."

He waited a little longer to see if she'd say more. She didn't.

"All right." He stood up. "Take the fries. Go home and get some sleep."

"I will."

"I'm here tomorrow if you want to talk about it."

"I know. Thanks, Benny."

She packed up the laptop. He held the door for her, then locked it behind them.

7

She meant to sleep. She got into bed and put the laptop on the blanket beside her to charge. A while later it was on her chest, and she was looking at the envelope again.

The cut-off line along the bottom bothered her. She zoomed in as far as the scan allowed. Four letters, maybe five, their bottoms lost off the edge. The pencil was softer and darker than the rest, and the hand was upright instead of slanted. Someone had written on that envelope later.

Not the observer, then. Someone who had taken the plate out afterward.

Plates were borrowed. Researchers wrote in for them, or came and sat with a magnifier, and the collection kept a record of who had looked. If someone had looked at this one, maybe they'd written about it.

She went back to the literature database and searched for the plate number. Nothing. People didn't usually cite plates by number, and the full text of older papers was patchy, scanned for some and only abstracts for others. She tried the date, March 1937, along with "patrol" and "double images." Nothing. She put the observer's phrase, "sky not keeping its place," in quotation marks on the off chance someone had quoted it. Nothing.

She tried something looser: patrol plates and image anomalies, limited to the years after the plate-taking started up again, when someone might have gone back through the old glass. Most of what came up was about variable stars. She went through it a page at a time, opening each abstract and closing it.

On the fourth page there was a short paper from 1971, in a small society journal she had never heard of, the kind that got folded into something larger decades ago. The title was dry: something about transient image anomalies in patrol photography and their possible relation to non-instrumental causes.

There was no abstract. The link to the full text went to a page that wouldn't load. She tried the journal's later publisher, which had taken over the back issues. The volume was there. She opened its table of contents and found the paper before it and the paper after it, and their page numbers ran straight on, as if nothing had ever been in between.

She went back to the database record. Cited zero times. The reference list, which the database usually extracted automatically, was empty.

She searched the title in quotation marks on the open web. Two hits, both automatically generated bibliography pages that listed the same missing page numbers and nothing else. A library catalog in Ohio had once held that volume of the journal. It was marked withdrawn.

None of that proved anything. Old papers got lost all the time. Journals folded, scans failed, small societies went under and took their back issues with them. She'd chased missing references before and found them in somebody's filing cabinet. But there was usually something: a second record, a mention in a review, a conference abstract with the same title. Here there was nothing at all.

She looked at the author line. There was one name on it.

Potter, E.

She lay there and looked at it until the screen dimmed, and she touched a key to bring it back.
