The car took a few minutes to stop sounding cold. She set the thermos in the cupholder, where it ticked faintly against the plastic on the turns, and pulled out onto the county road.

Out here the roads didn't go anywhere in a straight line. They followed the old field edges and the shore, bending around stone walls and stands of birch that had gone yellow all at once. The maples were further along, red at the tops and still holding on lower down. Leaves had collected in the low places along the shoulder, and when she passed, they lifted and fell back.

The sky was doing what it did this time of year. Most of it had gone soft and washed out, pale blue thinning to lavender toward the east, with a faint, chalky pink at the edges. But the sun was low behind her, and where it reached the clouds it set them going. They were long, flat clouds, lit underneath in hard orange and a red so deep it was almost dark, and the color moved along them as the sun dropped.

She kept looking up. She knew she shouldn't as much as she did. On the straight stretches she let herself, a glance at a time, and at the stop sign by the Tibbetts' farm she sat a few seconds longer than she needed to with the thermos in both hands, just looking. The coffee was still almost too hot.

Past the farm the road climbed and opened up. The pond lay off to the right, flat and bright, holding a copy of the sky, and the far shore was already in shadow, the trees there just one dark shape.

Somewhere over the trees ahead, something was wrong.

She couldn't have said where. There was nothing to point at. It was more like the feeling of a picture hung a little crooked in a room she knew well, before she'd found which picture it was. Her eyes kept going back to one part of the sky and finding nothing there, and then going back again anyway.

She slowed without deciding to.

Then it was easier to see, or harder. A patch of the sky had a sheen to it, like oil on a puddle, except there were no colors in it, only the sheen. Through it the sky was the right sky and also not quite. The blue was the blue around it, but it sat wrong, a little smeared, the way a thing looks when your eyes are watering, except her eyes weren't watering and the rest of the sky was sharp. One of the long clouds ran into it and stopped. It didn't thin out or fade. It just ended, along an edge she couldn't find when she looked for it.

She tried to take it in all at once and couldn't. Every time she settled on what it was, it stopped fitting that, and she had to start over. It felt less like looking at something and more like something had gone wrong with the looking.

She blinked hard, twice. It was still there, or the feeling was.

She leaned her head an inch to the left, then to the right, watching it against the windshield pillar. A smudge on the glass would have slid. It didn't. She thumbed the washer and let the wipers go across once. Nothing changed, except the glass was cleaner.

Heat shimmer, she thought. Layers of air at different temperatures bent light; she'd watched it make a dry road look wet. Over the pond, with the ground giving up its warmth, something like that could happen.

But shimmer moved, and this held still. And shimmer didn't cut a cloud off.

The road dropped into the trees, and the sky closed down to a strip between the branches. When it opened again at the bottom of the hill, she found the place, or where she thought the place had been. The cloud was whole, lit along its underside like the others. The sky behind it was only sky.

She drove for a while, glancing up anyway, and found she was holding the wheel more tightly than she needed to. She loosened her hands one at a time.

She tried to remember exactly what she had seen and found she couldn't put it together again. The sheen, the cloud, the edge. Each part was clear on its own, and they wouldn't go together. She couldn't tell anymore whether she'd seen something or only had the feeling of seeing it.

She reached for the thermos and drank without tasting much.

The sign for Blueberry Pond came up on the right, the paint half gone. She put on her blinker for no one and turned in, and the gravel took over from the pavement as the last of the red went out of the clouds.
