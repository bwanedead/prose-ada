Later, the shutter caught again on its way across to follow the field. It held longer than it had before, and the sound it made was worse.

"There it is," he said.

"I'm going," she said, and took the radio and the flashlight.

Jonas reached for the clipboard that hung by the dome door, the one for anyone going up, and looked at the clock while he wrote. "Twenty oh-four," he said. "Try not to fall off."

The ladder went up the inside of the dome wall to a narrow grated landing beside the slit, where a person could get a hand on the shutter track. It was colder up there. The slit was open beside her, a long strip of stars, and below her the telescope pointed out through it, not moving.

She put the light along the rail the shutter ran on. There was a bright scuff on one of the joints where the wheel had been catching. She reached up to feel it, and the thought came to her, plainly, that she should tell Jonas it was a burr.

Then it came again.

She should tell him it was a burr. She should tell him. It was a burr, she should tell him. It came around faster each time, the same few words, and she noticed it coming around, and noticed herself noticing, and the noticing came around too, so that she was both the one thinking it and the one watching it get thought, over and over, and couldn't get either of them to stop.

She tried to take her hand down from the steel. It didn't come.

Nothing in her moved. She could feel that she was shaking, a fine fast tremor all through her, in her jaw and her knees and the hand on the steel, and at the same time she could feel that she was perfectly still, and both of those were true and she couldn't make them into one thing.

The strip of sky had gone wrong. Not the way it had over the trees. This was nearer, everywhere at the edges of what she could see, a greasy kind of shimmer over the stars and the steel and her own hand, as if all of it were being shown to her through something that wasn't quite clear. The stars were where they should be. They were also very slightly not. When she tried to look at any one of them, it was there, and then there was another just beside it that was the same star, and she couldn't tell which one was in the sky.

She breathed in, and heard it. Then she heard it again, a little after, and again after that, softer, like the breath had gone out into a much bigger room than the dome and was coming back to her from the walls. She breathed out into the sound of breathing in.

She lost any sense of when she was. Not what time; she couldn't have said what that would mean anymore. She didn't know if she had just climbed the ladder or had been standing here a long while, or if she was about to climb it. She had the sense of having stood on this landing before, with her hand just like this, many times, and of the times being very close together, almost on top of one another, and of being pressed between them.

It got tighter. The air was the same air but there wasn't room in it. She wanted badly to move, to make any part of herself do one thing that was only hers, and she couldn't find the edge of herself to start from.

There was a feeling, under all of it, that she was in the way of something. That something was turning toward where she stood, unhurried, the way you turn toward a sound you aren't sure you heard.

It went on. It went on long enough that she stopped waiting for it to end. Long enough that some part of her settled into it, the way you settle into a long night, and she felt the cold of the landing soak all the way in, and her legs go heavy, and the trembling go on and on.

Then her hand came down.

She was standing on the landing. The stars were stars. The cold was just cold, on her face and through the back of her jacket. Below her the telescope hadn't moved.

She held the rail with both hands. Her legs didn't feel like hers. Her mouth was dry the way it was after a whole night's sleep, and her eyes ached as if she'd been staring at something for hours.

She looked at the rail, and at the flashlight in her hand, and couldn't remember what she had come up to do. She waited for it to come back. It didn't.

After a while she climbed down, slowly, making sure of each rung.
