She parked by the fence and turned the engine off, and then sat there.

The dome was up on the rise in front of her, pale against a sky going blue-grey, and the control room window was lit. Everything was where it always was. She found she had been looking at the sky over the trees again, and made herself pick up the thermos and go in.

Inside it was warm and smelled of carpet, old electronics, and somebody's microwaved soup.

Wade Tolliver was in the control room with his coat already on, leaning over the desk to finish typing something. Jonas Halme was in the other chair with his feet up on a box of printer paper, eating the soup.

"You're early," Wade said.

"A little."

Jonas glanced up from his soup, then back down. "Huh."

She set the thermos down by the keyboard, where it always went. Wade finished his sentence, read it back, and stood up.

"Okay. Biases are done if you want them, I took a set before I ate. Fans have been going since I got in, so the dome should be close to outside by the time you open. Humidity's dropping all night, wind's nothing. Should be clean." He pulled his hat out of his pocket. "Upper shutter hung up again when I tested it. Same spot. It went after a second, but somebody should look at it before it decides not to."

"Motor?"

"If I knew that I'd have fixed it." He pointed at Jonas. "He has a theory."

"It's not the motor," Jonas said.

"He also said the fan was the bearings."

"The fan was the bearings."

Wade looked at Ada as if she might settle it. She didn't. She was looking at the weather screen, the slow sweep of the radar, mostly empty, and it took her a second to notice they were both waiting.

"Sorry," she said. "I'll look at it."

"You good?" Wade said.

"I'm good. Long drive."

"It's twenty minutes."

"It was a long twenty minutes."

He let that go, and zipped his coat. "Miriam Holloway's program first, then the variable stars if you've got time at the end. She left a note. It's very polite, which means she really wants it." He picked up his lunch bag and stopped in the doorway. "Sky looked good on the way in?"

She thought about it, which she hadn't meant to do.

"It looked pretty," she said.

"Good. Have a quiet one."

His footsteps went down the hall, then the outer door, then his car on the gravel, and then it was just the hum and Jonas scraping the bottom of the soup container.

She sat down in the chair with the cracked armrest and pulled up the log. Wade's notes were short, as always. She read them twice, the second time actually reading them.

Then she opened the startup checklist, the same as every night, and started at the top.

The camera was already down at temperature, holding steady. She checked the disk, which had room for the night and then some, and that last night's data had gone off to the archive. She read Holloway's note. It was polite, as promised: a list of fields with the order she wanted them in, the exposure times, and a line at the bottom that said if the seeing was good, could they please, please get the third one. Ada moved the third one up to second.

"She'll expect that now," Jonas said, reading over her shoulder.

"She said please twice."

"That's how they get you."

Outside the window the last of the color was going out of the sky. Jonas turned off the fans and opened the shutter. The sound of it came down through the building, a long grinding hum, and stopped once, partway, for a beat. Then it went on.

They both listened to it.

"Not the motor," he said.

"I'll go up later."

The first stars came while they were taking flats, the telescope pointed a little east of overhead at the smooth part of the sky, the exposures getting longer each time as the twilight faded. When the frames started showing pinpricks, they stopped. Ada slewed to a bright star for the pointing check and it landed a hair off center, the way it did most nights, and she nudged it in and saved the offset. Then focus. She took a short frame, moved the focus a little, took another, and watched the star go from a soft blot to something small and hard and back again, and settled it at the hardest point. It took four tries.

She sent the telescope to Holloway's first field and started the sequence. The dome turned to follow, a low rumble that she felt more in the chair than heard. The first frame came in clean. She looked at it longer than she needed to. It was stars, the right stars, where they were supposed to be. She typed the start of the sequence into the log with the time from the clock on the wall, the way she did for everything, and sat back with the thermos.

Jonas went to see about more soup.

The frames kept coming in. Each one looked the way it was supposed to.
