[[[lock|KEEP|lock_1774426385172_gtedvo|start]]]The room was still too dark for anything in it to come clear at once. The furniture was vaguely there, but the darkness between did most of the work of holding it together — outlines suggested, interiors soft and vague, all of it washed in a faint red tint. Nothing separated sharply from anything else except a set of four red glowing numerals and, a short distance away, a point of reflection — an eye already open, staring not at the numbers but past them. The numerals were clearer in the reflection of Ada's pupil than in her actual attention, where the red numbers had blurred and blended out of focus. They'd become scenery, a backdrop for her first thoughts of the day to project against.

The alarm sounded. The red smears drifted back into digits.

She let it run — not in protest, and not because she wanted more sleep. Part of her was still elsewhere, following whatever thread had been running before the sound arrived, and she wasn't willing to drop it just because the clock insisted. She stayed where she was, still warm under the blanket, letting the sound exist without answering it.

After the pressure of annoyance grew past her tolerance, she reached out from the warmth and silenced it.

The small facts of herself started arriving one by one.

The air sat cool against her face. One shoulder gave complaint from having taken too much of her weight through the night. Her mouth was dry. Her bladder was making itself known.

She looked at the clock again, though she never needed the exact minute at first. It was enough to know the sequence had begun.

The shade over the window kept most of the outside back. Only a thin row of yellow light showed at the bottom where it didn't quite meet the sill, but it carried enough information anyway — sunny, calm, no surprises.

She pushed the blanket down and sat up.

Somewhere deeper in the apartment the boards answered, then the pipes. The place always seemed to register her in little delayed acknowledgments, as if it kept its own record of her routines.

She sat hunched a moment longer, elbows on her knees, knowing this was the last pause before starting the day.

Then she swung her legs over the side of the bed and found the rug with her toes. The corner nearest the door was arched upwards as it always was. She pressed it flat, knowing it would be curled again later.

[[[beat-02-morning-ritual|Morning Ritual|start]]]

There were things in her apartment that accepted correction only temporarily. The kitchen drawer that liked to stop just short of closed. The bathroom door that had to be lifted slightly at the knob. Ada had lived there long enough to stop treating these as flaws. They were just the terms.

She stood and waited for herself to gather, like coming online in layers. First the body's demands. Then the room. Then the larger fact of having a day attached to her again.

She pulled on the T-shirt hanging over the chair and stepped into the sweatpants she had left as a grey knot on the floor.

After the bathroom the lamp went on beside the couch. The room shifted to amber. Smaller. More complete somehow. The black in the corners thinned to a soft brown, and the shadows pulled in close behind the furniture. The surfaces remembered.

That helped.

She moved into the kitchen and filled the kettle at the sink. The metal gave a soft hollow sound under the stream. She set it on the stove, turned the burner on, and reached automatically for the coffee. The motions were old enough to feel pre-decided: filter, grounds, water soon after. Her hands went through them in order without asking her for anything, and she was glad to let them.

While the kettle heated, she leaned one hip against the counter.

The kettle started low, with a scattered sound that slowly filled in, like a stadium's roar building.

The shade over the sink was down, glowing a little at its edges. Ada looked at it for a while without seeing much. Bits of the night ahead began to come to her on their own: the drive, the cold of the dome, what was left over from yesterday's run. She let them come and go. There was no need to take any of it up yet.

When the kettle was ready, she poured slowly over the grounds and watched the bloom rise, darken, and sink. The smell reached her before the steam did. It came up in a wave, dark and roasted with something almost sweet under it, and she felt it go through her, waking up parts of her that had still been idling behind her eyes.

She stood there with one hand resting lightly on the counter, waiting for it to finish dripping, feeling herself come a little more fully into outline.

When the last drops had slowed and stopped, she dropped the filter in the trash and poured her mug.

As the coffee rose in the mug, the bottom went away. One moment there was a cup with a floor to it, and the next there was only dark, level and still, with no depth she could measure. The black mug exaggerated the illusion. She couldn't tell where the inside wall stopped and the coffee started. The edges of the surface didn't end so much as fade, giving way gradually until there was nothing to separate them from the rest. A few small bubbles clung along one side. They were the only thing breaking the evenness of it, the only proof that it had a surface at all.

She kept looking.

She wondered, briefly, whether it really was an illusion, and then she stopped wondering and let it be what it looked like. A hole in her hands with no bottom to it. Her eyes went looking for a floor and kept going. The counter and the lamp and the faint warmth of the room drifted out past the edges of her attention, and for a moment there was nothing but the dark and her, leaning a little closer to it. It was very quiet down there. It seemed to hold still for her, the way a deep thing would.

Then one of the bubbles popped, and the cup was a cup again. The kitchen closed back in around her, the counter under her hand, the lamp's amber at the edge of her vision, the night's work waiting somewhere just ahead. Everything settled back into its lines.

She breathed out through her nose, a little amused at herself.

She picked up the mug. The heat came through the ceramic and into her palms, almost too much, which was the right amount. The smell had gone soft by now. She had been standing in it long enough that it belonged to the room.

Then the cream. She tipped the carton and kept it tipped a beat longer than she should have. "Oops," she said to no one, and didn't pour any back.

It went in and vanished, then came back up from underneath, rolling outward in slow pale clouds that turned over on themselves before giving in and spreading until the whole thing went tan.

She took the mug to the chair by the lamp and folded herself into it, knees up, both hands around the warmth.

The first sip was too hot, and she took it anyway.

For a while she didn't think about anything in particular. Then the cream came back to her. Why sink first, then rise? Cream was fatty, and fat floats, but it had come straight out of the fridge, and cold things are heavier than the same thing warm. So the cold had dragged it down, and the pour had helped. Then the coffee warmed it from the bottom up until it was lighter than what was around it, and up it came. She turned it over once more and found nothing wrong with it, and was pleased to have settled something before work.

From the chair she could see the counter, and the thin line of light coming in at the bottom edge of the shade, lying at a slant across it. Near the far end was a pale spot in the laminate, an old ring left by something set down too hot, too long ago to remember what. A while back she had noticed that when the corner of that light reached the spot, it was about time for her to leave. It drifted with the seasons, and she adjusted for that without really thinking about it.

She drank slowly and checked it now and then. The light never seemed to move while she watched it. It was only a little farther along each time she looked back.

The last of the coffee was cooler and sweeter than the rest.

When she looked again, the corner of the light had reached the pale spot and was resting on its edge.

She'd meant to get in a little early tonight anyway. She got up and poured what was left in the pot into the thermos, creamed it the same way, and screwed the lid down tight. She rinsed the mug and set it upside down on the cloth by the sink.

Her keys were on the hook, her bag by the door, her jacket over the back of the couch. She took each one up on the way past without stopping, shrugging the jacket on with the thermos tucked under one arm.

She opened the door.

The warmth of the apartment ended right at the threshold. Outside was a wall of low, bright light and a crisp chill that met her face first, then found its way through the jacket to her chest, and ran down through her in a fine shiver, out along every nerve to the ends of her fingers.
